# The Clarity Framework

A reusable structure for defining any offering, playbook, or productized service with high clarity of thought. Answer seven questions well and the offering is sharp: sales can pitch it, delivery can scope it, and it stops drifting. The medium (one-pager, deck, SOW, wiki page) is a downstream concern; nail the answers first and the content writes itself.

Work the questions in this order. The audience constrains the definition, so "who" comes first for reasoning. State our own motive plainly and keep it separate from the client's.

1. Who is it for?
2. What is it?
3. Why should they care? (the client)
4. Why should we care? (us)
5. What's included?
6. What's not included?
7. How do we deliver it?

Note the symmetry in 3 and 4: same question, once for the client and once inverted for us. Keep both. If you answer only the client's, our intent stays implicit and the offering drifts.

## 1. Who is it for?

Define the audience by posture and situation, not just firmographics. The sharpest definitions name the state the buyer is in and what makes them qualified, and they name the disqualifiers at both ends.

- Separate who you **design for** (the economic buyer, the person who can say yes) from who you **accept** (a more junior sponsor who is a credible path to that buyer). Design for the buyer; never for the influencer.
- State the profile (size, segment) as part of the definition when it changes what converts, not as a footnote.

Trap: defining the audience so broadly it qualifies no one. If it includes everyone, it is not a qualifier.

## 2. What is it?

One sentence that makes complete sense in a vacuum, to someone with zero context. Then name the category you are playing in and what makes you different.

- State what it is **not** at the definitional level. This is positioning, not scope (that is question 6). For example: "not a broad strategy review," "not an audit."
- Choose the category word deliberately. It sets the buyer's expectation before they read anything else.

Trap: cryptic internal shorthand ("a focused opportunity read") that means nothing to an outsider. Write the first sentence for someone who has never heard of it.

## 3. Why should they care? (the client)

The client's motivation, in the client's language. Lead with the outcome and the change in their situation, not with features or what you do.

- If there are multiple buyers, state one unified core, then a short emphasis per buyer. Tune depth by audience, never tone.
- Watch for the most common failure: stating why *we* care and calling it client value. "It builds our pipeline" is question 4, not this one.

Trap: features dressed as benefits. Name the pain it removes or the result it creates.

## 4. Why should we care? (us)

Our objective, stated plainly. What does this convert to or lead to?

- For anything free, discounted, or loss-leading, this is the **design driver**. Reverse-engineer every other answer from what you want it to lead to. If you leave the intent implicit, scope inflates and the thing stops pulling toward the next step.
- Include the handoff: what leaves the client's hands and enters ours.

Trap: skipping this because it feels obvious. Unstated intent is how offerings lose their commercial point.

## 5. What's included?

The concrete scope: the content, the activities, the deliverables. Specific enough that delivery can estimate it and the client knows exactly what they get.

Trap: vagueness. "A thorough analysis" is not scope. Name the artifacts and the activities.

## 6. What's not included?

The boundaries. This is usually the thinnest section and the most valuable, because it is where scope creep and liability live.

- Governing principle: **every boundary doubles as a doorway.** State each limit as the on-ramp to the paid next step. "We don't do X here. That is the next engagement." One sentence, two jobs: it protects capacity and seeds the conversion, and it keeps the artifact from becoming a wall of disclaimers.
- Distinguish client-facing exclusions (what they don't get) from internal guardrails (capacity, time caps). Both matter; they read differently.
- Give the boundaries a contractual home (an SOW or letter of engagement), or they are just good intentions.

Trap: leaving it thin. A sharp "what it's not" sharpens every other answer.

## 7. How do we deliver it?

The mechanics: format, flow, effort, who runs it, how long. The medium is a secondary and often variable concern, so decide it last and don't let it drive the definition.

- Keep the **handoff / what-happens-next** out of here. That belongs with question 4. This question is delivery mechanics only.

Trap: bundling delivery with conversion. They are different questions.

## Maturity marking

Mark each answer so the team knows what is settled:

- **locked**: worked through and settled
- **open**: still needs scrutiny

An offering is not ready to sell until all seven are locked. Gaps almost always cluster in 3 (client value), 4 (our intent), and 6 (boundaries), so check those first.

Use the words, not a symbol. A glyph does not survive a paste into a doc, a CRM field, or a diff, and a score would imply a precision that does not exist.

## Reasoning order vs presentation order

The order above (Who first) is the **reasoning** order: how you think the offering through, because the audience constrains the definition.

For **downstream presentation artifacts** (one-pagers, decks, pages), lead with **What is it?** People need to know what they are reading about before anything else lands. So keep thinking docs in reasoning order (Who then What), and lead client- and sales-facing artifacts with What, then Who, then Why they care.
