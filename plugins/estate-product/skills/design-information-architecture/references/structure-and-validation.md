# Structure and validation

## Inspect the current structure

Inventory the content within the requested scope. Retain each item's title and current URL or route. Record its page type and audience need, plus its existing parent or navigation placement. Record duplicates only after comparing content and purpose. Similar titles alone do not justify removal.

Identify how people enter: a home page, external link, saved URL, search result, or an in-product route. Use actual analytics or research if supplied. Otherwise mark entry-path assumptions as proposed. A navigation tree cannot explain every route through a product.

## Choose a structure

Use hierarchy when content has a clear parent-child relationship and readers can predict that relationship. Use facets when people need to combine independent attributes such as topic and format. Search can support known-item retrieval or a large inventory; it does not remove the need for clear labels and result context.

Describe page types by their purpose and required content. Keep URLs stable and readable within the product's established conventions. Do not add a universal depth limit or menu-item count. Assess whether a reader can understand the grouping and complete the intended path.

For each proposed grouping, record its user purpose and the content it contains. Give ambiguous items a rationale or alternative placement. Preserve a route to important content that does not belong in the primary menu.

## Preserve links when proposing change

A structural redesign may change navigation without changing URLs. When a URL change is necessary, map each affected current URL to its proposed destination. State the redirect requirement and identify internal links needing an update. Retain incoming-link and saved-link behavior in the plan. Identify merged or retired content explicitly. Do not claim a redirect works until its deployed behavior has been checked.

## Select a validation method

Card sorting can inform groupings and label vocabulary. Record who participated, which items they sorted, and the method. Disagreement may reveal different needs; a popular grouping is not automatically the only correct one.

A tree test checks whether participants can find a destination in a proposed hierarchy without the visual interface. Use goal-based tasks and a defined target. Preserve observed paths and success counts. Report uncertainty in those results. A first-click test checks the starting choice on an actual or proposed interface; it measures a different stage of the path.

Choose the method for the uncertainty being resolved. Keep sample limits and uncovered audiences visible. Without participant evidence, deliver a provisional structure and the tasks needed to evaluate it. Do not invent a validation rate or call an agent walkthrough a user study.
