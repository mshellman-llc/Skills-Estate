# Design information architecture

Make the product's content findable for the audience's actual tasks. Read the current content inventory and navigation before proposing a structure. Use the supplied product scope; do not reorganize private source folders or change their authority routes.

Read [Structure and validation](references/structure-and-validation.md). Identify the audience's goals, available content, page types, and existing entry points. Keep required content and unknown inventory visible. Group by a supported user need rather than the organization's departments or an arbitrary number of menu items.

Choose a hierarchy, facets, search, or a combination that suits the content. Give labels enough meaning to help a reader predict the destination. Separate primary navigation from page-level or contextual links when their jobs differ. Show representative paths through the proposed structure.

Preserve existing public URLs unless a change is necessary and authorized. If a proposal changes them, provide a current-to-proposed map and identify the redirect and link-update work. A proposed map does not prove that redirects exist or a migration is deployed.

Treat card sorting, tree tests, and first-click studies as participant research. Report validation only when actual observations support it. An agent review can identify ambiguity or inconsistent labels; it cannot stand in for users. Without research, label the structure provisional and give a focused validation plan.

Deliver the requested sitemap or structural recommendations with reasons tied to the audience and inventory. Show content gaps that affect the paths. Preserve confirmed names and labels unless the user requested changing them. Existing brand, source, writing, and completion rules govern any finished artifact.
