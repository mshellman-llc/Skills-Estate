# Session design

## Turn a decision into tasks

State what the study should help decide: for example, whether shoppers can find delivery information before committing to checkout. Use known product facts in the scenario. If delivery terms are unknown, leave that detail pending rather than supplying a fictitious policy.

A participant task might say: “You need this order by Friday. Find out whether it can arrive in time.” The moderator's completion condition might be: “Participant locates the relevant delivery estimate and explains whether it meets the deadline.” The participant should not hear the control label or expected route.

Choose tasks that cover the decision without exhausting the participant. Use a safe test environment for checkout or account changes. Identify the stopping point before a charge, submission, or irreversible action.

## Moderate without teaching

Explain the study's purpose and the right to stop. Let the participant choose whether to be recorded under the approved study process. State that the interface is being tested. Confirm consent before recording.

Give the scenario and observe. Neutral prompts include “What are you looking for?” and “What would you do next?” Avoid hints such as “Did you see the delivery tab?” Record any assistance; an assisted completion is different from an unassisted one. Define when to stop a stalled task in the plan, and retain elapsed time when actually measured.

## Keep an evidence record

For each observation, retain the participant code, task, product version, behavior, outcome, assistance, and source location. Add a verbatim quote only when present in the source and useful to explain the problem. Distinguish moderator notes from recordings. Resolve conflicting notes explicitly.

Report counts as observed counts: “2 of the 4 participants who attempted the task needed a hint.” Do not generalize that fraction to all customers. Do not report a completion time that was not recorded. A participant's preference does not establish that a design performs better.

## Decide what to repair

Describe the barrier and affected goal before assigning priority. Assess the harm and whether the participant can recover. Report how often the problem occurred in this sample. An irreversible mistake or exclusion can justify high priority after one observation. Label inferred causes as hypotheses; a failure does not automatically prove its root cause.

Group observations only when their mechanism is supported. Keep contradictory cases visible. Recommend a specific change or further test tied to the decision. For retesting, reuse comparable goals and completion conditions; record changes in participants and product state.

## With missing evidence

With a product brief and no participants, propose the tasks and recruitment criteria. Prepare a moderation plan with an observation record. Mark study results pending. With incomplete notes, synthesize only the visible evidence and identify the missing task coverage. A simulated walkthrough can supply hypotheses for the next study, never participant findings.
