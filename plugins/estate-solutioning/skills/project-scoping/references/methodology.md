# Scoping and estimation method

State the work in terms the project owner can check. Use a short explanation
for each item, followed by the actions or conditions needed to define it.
Keep the source link beside the item in the working model. A template should
not force a paragraph or a bullet count.

## Describe scope and assumptions

Explain the behavior supplied and its boundary. For example, a lead-assignment
item can define matching rules and fallback handling. Its acceptance conditions
need to cover an unmatched lead. A general promise to improve routing does not
define that work.

Use full item names in cross-references, with stable IDs in the underlying
model. Keep business goals in the overview while retaining the relationship
between those goals and the scoped behavior.

An assumption is a proposed condition used for estimating. State its source
and effect if it changes. If prior documentation is unavailable to the agent,
do not assert that it does not exist. Record that its availability is unknown
and explain how obtaining it could affect the work.

## Calibrate effort

Use relevant historical observations where available. Record the sample and
its scope differences before applying a median or interquartile range.
Sparse observations may support a comparable, but a sample-count threshold
does not establish statistical confidence by itself.

Decompose the work by role and lifecycle activity. A project preset may supply
senior/junior ratios or indirect labor. Read those values from current
configuration, explain their source and check for double counting. No ratio
or phase percentage is supplied by this estate.

For an anchor such as two weeks of development, obtain the intended weekly
hours and availability. Calculate the anchored effort before estimating the
remaining work. Do not infer a 40-hour week or a standard phase split.

Keep the expected estimate between its low and high bounds. Explain the
scope or complexity conditions that move it. Preserve an unestimated item
when the basis is absent; do not silently exclude it from a project total.

## Reconcile workload and staffing

Bottom-up effort describes the work by item and role. A staffing timeline
places available people against that work over time. Reconcile their hours
and explain differences, including shared oversight or support. A custom
pricing system may calculate the quote from its active timeline; verify that
behavior before treating it as the commercial authority.

Compare variance with thresholds confirmed for the project. Preserve the
actual difference even when no threshold is configured. Never import an old
warning or error percentage as a universal rule.

Role and seniority may affect different rates. Use the rate-card fields the
project actually maintains. A staffing change can affect cost and technical
risk differently; do not assume seniority alone establishes either result.

## Build a feasible timeline

Use available hours per role to calculate a workload lower bound. Then place
the work against its dependencies and access conditions. Account for reviews
and expected client response time when supported. Without those inputs, a
capacity calculation is a bound rather than a committed schedule.

Check resource continuity when the delivery model requires it. Review role
oversight and availability through testing. Respect confirmed staffing
increments, but do not invent mandatory architect pairings or assume every
resource remains allocated through every phase.

Retain alternate staffing scenarios as separate revisions. Compare their
hours and cost with the same scope source. A scenario becomes an accepted
commitment only through the project's actual commercial decision.
