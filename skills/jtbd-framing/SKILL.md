# Jobs-to-be-Done Framing

> **Before writing: [`build-card.md`](../_shared/build-card.md) plus
> [`readout-brief.md`](../prose-pass/references/registers/readout-brief.md). Inputs, not a final pass.**

Jobs-to-be-Done as applied methodology. Job statements, struggling moments, hire and fire criteria, the difference between feature-thinking and job-thinking. Honest about where JTBD adds clarity and where it becomes performative ritual.

JTBD is one of the more cited and less practiced frameworks in product. Teams cite it in strategy docs, run job-statement workshops, produce wall-sized artifacts, and keep building from feature requests and persona archetypes the next quarter. The methodology gets the credit; the practice gets skipped.

The framework's actual contribution is surfacing what users are trying to ACCOMPLISH, rather than treating them as preference-aggregators (feature requests) or demographic archetypes (persona theater). Grounded in struggling moments and hire/fire criteria, it produces decisions. Stopped at the job-statement worksheet, it produces ritual. This skill is honest about both modes.

Use it when applying JTBD to a discovery cycle, replacing persona-driven prioritization with job-driven prioritization, reframing positioning around what users hire the product to do, or auditing whether existing JTBD work is driving decisions.

A hard rule: jobs come from data. Struggling moments, hire criteria, and fire criteria are grounded in what users actually said or did, not invented to fit a hypothesis. When no research exists yet, produce candidate jobs labeled as hypotheses and the interview prompts to test them; do not fabricate specific quotes, frequencies, or moments.

---

## Scope

In scope: JTBD as a framing technique within product work, its strengths, and its failure modes. The audience is PMs, product directors, product strategists, and teams considering or already using JTBD.

Out of scope: other product strategy frameworks (Wardley mapping, North Star, OKRs); the broader research synthesis discipline that JTBD is one technique within; the demographic-persona work that sometimes gets confused with JTBD. This skill hands off to a broader synthesis practice upstream, to spec writing and roadmap planning downstream (specs and roadmaps reference jobs as input), and to positioning and brand work where JTBD informs but does not replace creative direction.

---

## Feature-request-list vs persona-theater vs job-framing

The keystone framing.

**Feature-request-list.** "Users want X, Y, Z." Treats users as preference-aggregators. The team builds the most-requested features. Output: a product that satisfies stated preferences but misses what users were actually trying to accomplish. Users do not always know what would solve their problem; they describe symptoms, not solutions. Feature-list-driven products often improve incrementally without becoming meaningfully better.

**Persona-theater.** Demographic personas with cute names. "Marketing Manager Maria, 35, urban, Pinterest user, drinks oat milk lattes." Decorative, not decision-driving. Persona characteristics rarely correlate with what the user needs from the product; demographic detail substitutes for behavioral insight. Personas in the wild often live as wall posters nobody references in prioritization debates.

**Job-framing.** Users hire products to do jobs. The job is what the user is trying to accomplish, not who they are or what features they request. The framing surfaces struggling moments (when do users feel pulled toward an alternative), hire criteria (what makes them adopt), fire criteria (what makes them switch away). Output: product decisions grounded in user motivation rather than user description.

The litmus test. Take a decision the team is debating. Can JTBD ground it? "Should we build feature X?" Reframed: "What job would feature X help users do? What job is currently done badly that this addresses?" If the JTBD framing produces clearer arguments on both sides, the framing is earning its keep. If it just adds vocabulary without resolving the debate, it is ritual.

## The four mechanics

Each has a reference carrying the worked examples and the failure modes. What
follows is the part that changes what you do.

**Job statement.** "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."
The situation is a moment, never a demographic. All three levels have to be
specific or the statement drives nothing.
See [`references/job-statement-structure-patterns.md`](references/job-statement-structure-patterns.md).

**Struggling moments.** Jobs become visible where the current approach breaks.
Ask for the last specific time, not the general habit. "Users want to be more
productive" is not a struggling moment; "I lost an hour on Tuesday assembling
board metrics from three dashboards because none had the slice I needed" is.
Across users, these cluster, and the clusters are the jobs the market does badly.
See [`references/identifying-struggling-moments.md`](references/identifying-struggling-moments.md).

**Hire and fire criteria.** Why they adopt, why they leave. The two are usually
different, which is the whole point: what wins adoption is not what prevents
churn. Recent switch decisions are the richest source, because people remember
the moment. See [`references/hire-and-fire-criteria.md`](references/hire-and-fire-criteria.md).

**Three dimensions.** Functional (what they are accomplishing), emotional (how
they want to feel doing it), social (how they want to be seen doing it). Most
first-draft statements are functional only, and the other two are often where the
decision actually turns. Do not force all three onto a job that is genuinely
functional.
See [`references/functional-emotional-social-dimensions.md`](references/functional-emotional-social-dimensions.md).

## Where JTBD adds clarity vs where it becomes ritual

The honest framing.

**Where JTBD genuinely earns its keep.**

- **Discovery.** When research is grounded in struggling moments and hire/fire decisions, JTBD surfaces the user motivation that feature-list discovery misses. Interviews structured around jobs produce richer data than ones structured around feature preferences.
- **Prioritization.** When the team debates which work to prioritize, jobs ground the debate in what users are trying to accomplish. "Does this help users do an important job better?" cuts through the politics of feature-request prioritization.
- **Positioning.** When articulating what the product does, jobs frame it as a way of accomplishing something the customer cares about, rather than as a feature list.

**Where JTBD becomes ritual.**

- **Job-statement workshops as deliverables.** The team runs a workshop, produces a wall of job statements, and never references them in subsequent decisions. The workshop became the deliverable; the analytical work did not happen.
- **Job statements substituted for personas without rigor.** "We have JTBD now" but the team treats job statements as named characters (Job Bob, Job Alice) instead of as analytical tools.
- **Mandatory JTBD for every question.** Every meeting opens with "what's the job?" even where the framing adds nothing. The method becomes a tax, not a tool.
- **JTBD as compliance.** Adopted because a book endorsed it, and artifacts get produced to show the framework is in use; nobody decides anything with it.

**The honest signal.** If a recent decision went differently because of the analysis, the framework is earning its keep. Vocabulary with no traceable decision behind it is ritual.

## Boundary with `discovery-research-synthesizer`

That skill is the pipeline from raw call to structured artifacts, and job
statements are one output. This is the bar they are held to.

## Where to apply it

**Discovery.** Structure interviews around recent struggling moments and real
switch decisions. Gather job statements from the data afterwards, never from a
whiteboard beforehand. Cluster across users, then name the jobs from the clusters.
See [`references/applying-jtbd-to-discovery.md`](references/applying-jtbd-to-discovery.md).

**Prioritization.** Frame each roadmap candidate by the job it addresses, then ask
which jobs the product currently does badly. That question cuts through
feature-request politics better than scoring does.
See [`references/applying-jtbd-to-prioritization.md`](references/applying-jtbd-to-prioritization.md).

**Positioning.** Lead with the job, not the features. "Help product teams turn
discovery research into decisions" is job framing. "AI-powered research synthesis
with collaboration tools" is feature framing.
See [`references/applying-jtbd-to-positioning.md`](references/applying-jtbd-to-positioning.md).

## Common failure modes

Eleven, with diagnoses, in [`references/common-jtbd-failures.md`](references/common-jtbd-failures.md).
The four that account for most of it:

- **"We ran the workshop but nothing changed."** The workshop became the
  deliverable. The analytical work never happened.
- **"Our job statements all sound the same."** Not grounded in specific moments.
  Re-ground them in interview data.
- **"Our jobs read like feature requests."** "When using product X, I want feature
  Y" is not a job. The job is what they were trying to accomplish, independent of
  your product.
- **"We cannot agree on the job."** Usually different segments with different jobs.
  Surface the segment split instead of averaging it away.

## The audit: 12 checks

Walk these when applying JTBD or reviewing someone's JTBD work.

1. Job-framing, not feature-list or persona-theater.
2. Situation, motivation, outcome all specific.
3. Grounded in specific recent struggling moments.
4. Hire criteria identified.
5. Fire criteria identified, and different from hire.
6. Functional, emotional and social surfaced where they matter.
7. Jobs derived from data, not from a workshop.
8. Applied where it earns its keep, not everywhere.
9. Segment differences surfaced rather than averaged.
10. Each major decision traceable to the analysis.
11. Vocabulary not substituted for analytical work.
12. Honest about where the framing does not help.
